About

TOT Insights is a research platform on Russia's occupation of Ukrainian territory, based in the Ukraine and Russia Programme at the Centre for Statecraft and National Security, King's College London. It exists to bring rigorous, evidence-based analysis to a subject that is usually reported from a distance: what is actually happening inside the occupied territories, and how the occupation works.

We build that analysis with — and around — people from the occupied territories themselves. And we address it primarily to audiences outside Ukraine, where understanding of the occupation is thinnest and most consequential: in scholarship, policy, media, and public debate.

Our objectives
  1. Produce and encourage research. To carry out original research on the occupied territories and to build the knowledge base that other researchers can draw on.
  2. Inform policy. To broaden and deepen policy-level discussion of the occupied territories with grounded, verifiable analysis.
  3. Co-operate — especially with Ukrainians displaced from the occupied territories. To work with others who study the occupied territories, and above all with people from those territories, so that discussion in academia, policy, media, and the wider public outside Ukraine is better informed.
How the platform works

The platform works in three connected ways.

  1. A public site, to inform. We publish analytical briefings, selected datasets, cleared primary-source material, and visualisations openly, so that anyone working on the occupied territories can draw on them. This is where our research reaches the scholars, policymakers, journalists, and readers who act on it.
  2. Closed repositories, as the research base. Behind the public analysis sits a fuller body of research material — datasets, primary-source collections, and monitoring records. These repositories are closed rather than open, chiefly to protect the people and sources behind them, and we share material from them selectively with trusted researchers and partners.
  3. A network of partners. We are a collaborative platform, not a closed one. We work with a network of organisations — and with experts on the occupied territories, including people from those territories — to share data, pursue joint research, and connect others to the right expertise. Partner organisations sometimes co-publish their work with us here, credited to them.

Correspondence is welcome in English, Ukrainian, or Russian.

If you would like to get in touch, please email jade.mcglynn@kcl.ac.uk.

Methodology & ethics

We work to explicit standards for how we reason and how we source. In short: we represent every position by its strongest form, we separate fact from assessment from opinion, we prefer documents and first-hand witnesses, and we are careful and transparent about the people and sources behind our work.

Read our full standards
How we reason
  1. We steelman. When we represent any actor's position — including one we find distasteful — we give its strongest version and its own best arguments, not a caricature.
  2. We separate fact, assessment, and opinion. A fact can be verified; an assessment is a conclusion drawn from facts and logic; an opinion is a subjective judgement. Our analysis carries facts and reasoned conclusions. Opinion appears only in clearly labelled commentary, never dressed up as analysis.
  3. We prefer documents to recollection, and witnesses to hearsay. Where testimony conflicts with documents, we favour documents. We rely on first-hand accounts and do not repeat rumour, however senior its source.
  4. We resist false patterns. Simultaneity is not coordination; sequence is not causation; a meeting is not a conspiracy; holding a post is not directing every act beneath it; benefiting from an outcome is not proof of causing it. We establish each link before asserting a pattern, rather than “connecting the dots.”
  5. We say when the picture is incomplete. Where we cannot establish the full picture, we say so plainly and explain what is missing.
How we source, and what we publish

The TOT Insights Hub holds King's College London research ethics approval (ref. LRS-25/26-55904); King's College London is the data controller. This section sets out how we gather, source, and present our research.

Evidence base. Our research draws on publicly available material and on systematic monitoring, and involves no primary data collection from individual people. Our sources are: publicly available institutional records (Russian government procurement portals, official publications, court records, occupation-administration documents); systematic monitoring of public Telegram channels operated by Russian military and occupation-administration bodies, where we read publicly posted content without interacting with the channels and without monitoring any private communications; data shared by registered Ukrainian NGO and research-organisation partners through secure encrypted transfer; and secondary analysis of existing anonymised datasets. We distinguish documented fact from our own assessment, and label analysis as such.

What we publish, and what we hold back. Not everything we hold is published. Each item is either published or kept in our restricted repository and used only to inform analysis. We publish content only where it is verified and sourced, where publishing it creates no risk of identifying any individual (including through the combination of separate data points), and where it adds genuine analytical value. We never publish individual-level data on civilians in occupied territory: survey data, data on children, and citizen-appeals data are published only in aggregated, anonymised form, with re-identification risk assessed before publication.

How we cite sources. Every factual claim is attributed. Publicly available sources are cited with a direct link to the specific material. Datasets we have deposited for citation are referenced by their institutional source and a DOI. Material from our closed monitoring is cited by a named, accountable institutional source, either "TOT Insights closed monitoring" or, where it comes from a partner, "Centre for the Study of Occupation monitoring," and is not linked.

Occupation and Russian state sources. We cite occupation and Russian state-controlled sources as evidence of what those bodies themselves say and do, never as independent confirmation of fact. In line with our research ethics, we do not provide direct links to occupied-territory occupation sources; we cite them by name and date, without directing readers or traffic to the occupier's own platforms, and link to a preserved archive copy where one exists. Russian national state sources are cited and linked as normal.

Naming individuals. We name an individual only where they are a public official acting in an official capacity, or where they are subject to formal sanctions, and we cite the source in each case. When we name a specific living person or company, we attribute allegations to their source rather than state them as settled fact, and we do not describe a named individual as a criminal absent a conviction. We process this data on the basis of the research exemption (Article 89 UK GDPR; Schedule 2, Data Protection Act 2018) and, where relevant, legitimate interests in accountability. UK data protection law applies to everyone regardless of conduct. We publish public-role information (name, position, appointment, and documented official decisions and actions); information beyond the public role is held in our restricted repository and published only where a specific accountability justification is documented. We do not treat listings on non-governmental accountability databases as a basis for naming anyone. Data shared by partner organisations is attributed to the organisation, not to individuals, unless an individual has given explicit written consent.

Closed monitoring and corrections. Some material is not published openly, to protect people in occupied territories and those who contribute to our work. We welcome corrections, and data subjects may exercise their rights under UK data protection law, by contacting jade.mcglynn@kcl.ac.uk.

Research team
Dr Jade McGlynn
Head of the Ukraine and Russia Programme, Centre for Statecraft and National Security, King's College London

Jade McGlynn is Head of the Ukraine and Russia Programme at the Centre for Statecraft and National Security, King's College London, and a Leverhulme Early Career Fellow. Her research centres on Russian-occupied Ukraine: the mechanisms of occupation, civilian entrapment and detention, and resistance in the occupied territories, alongside Russian information operations and memory politics. She conducts regular fieldwork in government-controlled eastern Ukraine (Kyiv, Kharkiv, Dnipro).

She has worked with Ukraine's Coordination Headquarters on the cases of civilians detained in the Russian Federation, and has advised European militaries and defence ministries on resistance and irregular warfare. Her recent work includes research on resistance in the occupied territories and on the deportation of Ukrainian children.

She holds a DPhil from the University of Oxford and is the author of Memory Makers (Bloomsbury, 2023) and Russia's War (Polity, 2023). She holds non-resident fellowships at CSIS in Washington, DC, ICDS in Tallinn, and the Kennan Institute.

Dr Maeve Ryan is a Reader in History and Foreign Policy at the Centre for Statecraft and National Security (CSNS), based within the Department of War Studies at King's College London. Vladyslav Havrylov
Research Consultant, Leverhulme Centre for Research on Slavery in War, King's College London

Vladyslav Havrylov is a Ukrainian historian and religious scholar based in Kyiv. His research interests include investigations of Russian war crimes against Ukrainian children, the history of mass deportations in the Soviet era, and state–confessional relations. Since October 2025 he has collaborated with King's College London as a research consultant at the Leverhulme Centre for Research on Slavery in War.

Havrylov was an analyst at the Media Initiative for Human Rights (September 2024 – January 2026), where he focused on crimes committed against prisoners of war and civilian detainees on the territory of the Russian Federation. He is also an invited expert with the charity foundation East-SOS, where from September 2024 to September 2025 he worked on investigations into Russian war crimes against Ukrainian children following the full-scale Russian invasion. He further serves as an adviser to the child-deportation investigative task force at OSINT for Ukraine.

Havrylov is a 2026 Global Fellow with the Collaborative on Global Children's Issues at Georgetown University, where he was previously a 2024–2025 Collaborative Fellow and a 2023–2024 Research Fellow. His work there has focused on the forcible transfer, deportation, adoption, and re-education of Ukrainian children by Russia.

Research Assistant
