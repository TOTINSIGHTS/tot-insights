1. Almenda Centre for Civil Education. Identity under Occupation: Report on Russian Policies and Practices in Occupied Ukrainian Territories. Kyiv: Almenda, 2025.

Documents Russian-installed authorities' claims of having subjected at least 48 minors in occupied Donetsk to mandatory psychiatric treatment on accusations of 'extremism' — including for listening to Ukrainian songs or expressing pro-Ukrainian views — providing rare documentary evidence of the punitive psychiatric dimension of identity suppression among children in occupied territory, distinct from but complementary to Almenda's separate camp-mapping and propaganda-curriculum reports elsewhere in this section.

Access source →

2. Amnesty International. Research Briefing: Russia/Ukraine: 10 Years of Occupation of Crimea. London: Amnesty International, March 2024.

A research briefing documenting a decade of Russian occupation practices in Crimea, including forced citizenship imposition, demographic engineering through Russian settlement, and — with direct relevance to children — the broader architecture of identity suppression (suppression of the Mejlis, Crimean Tatar cultural institutions, and Ukrainian-language education) within which the post-2022 child deportation system has clear institutional roots. Cites the 341 'transfer orders' issued against Ukrainians deemed foreigners in Crimea in 2022 alone as a direct continuation of the 2014-era population-management toolkit later scaled up against children in mainland occupied territory.

3. Amnesty International. Ukraine: 'Like a Prison Convoy': Russia's Unlawful Transfer and Abuse of Civilians in Ukraine during 'Filtration'. EUR 50/6136/2022. London: Amnesty International, 10 November 2022.

A dedicated Amnesty International report on the legal classification of Russia's filtration system as unlawful transfer and abuse of civilians, providing an authoritative international NGO assessment that complements the Yale HRL filtration-mapping report and Media Initiative for Human Rights documentation elsewhere in this section, with specific attention to how filtration procedures produced parent-child separations later channelled into the child deportation pipeline.

4. Amnesty International. Ukraine: Children's Education Is One More Casualty of Russian Aggression. EUR 50/7508/2023. London: Amnesty International, 2023.

Documents cases in which Ukrainian children in occupied territory were warned that speaking Ukrainian or expressing pro-Ukrainian sentiments could result in punishment or physical harm, and records testimony of a child punished by isolation in a dark room at a Crimean camp for crying for their parents — direct evidence linking educational disruption, linguistic suppression, and psychological punishment as interlocking features of the same occupation-education system.

5. Associated Press (Lori Hinnant, Lex Talamo, Lekan Oyekanmi); republished in PBS News. 'How Moscow Takes Ukrainian Children and Makes Them Russians.' October 13, 2022.

The most extensive investigation to date, as of its publication, following the full child-deportation pipeline from Ukraine through to children already living with Russian families, based on dozens of interviews and document review on both sides of the border. Documents the 2014 precedent directly: over 80 children stopped and abducted from Luhansk at checkpoints, an ECtHR case establishing they were transferred 'without medical support or the necessary paperwork,' and roughly 30 Crimean children adopted by Russians under the Train of Hope programme — some of whom, by 2022, human rights defender Kateryna Rashevska suggested might already be serving as Russian soldiers.

Analyses the US House of Representatives' 390–9 passage of a March 2024 resolution declaring that 'the Russian Federation is attempting to wipe out a generation of Ukrainian children' and that the abduction, transfer, and forcible adoption programme is 'contrary to Russia's obligations under the Genocide Convention and amounts to genocide' — formalising, via a near-unanimous bipartisan congressional vote, a legal characterisation previously confined to advocacy and scholarly literature.

12. Aydıngün, İsmail. 'Beyond Genocide, Ethnocide and Identicide: Russia's 'Strategic Imperial Demographic Policy' of Russifying Ukrainian Children.' Nations and Nationalism (2025): 1–11.

A peer-reviewed article using frame analysis of Ukrainian and Russian state narratives to argue that existing legal-conceptual categories — genocide, ethnocide, identicide — do not fully capture the phenomenon of Russia's child transfer programme, and proposes the alternative framing of a 'strategic imperial demographic policy.' Engages directly with the Yale HRL evidence base and situates the Ukrainian case within broader comparative genocide and nationalism studies, making it a key theoretical complement to the legal literature elsewhere in this section.

13. Azarov, Denis, Dmytro Koval, Gaiane Nuridzhanian, and Volodymyr Venher. 'Understanding Russia's Actions in Ukraine as the Crime of Genocide.' Journal of International Criminal Justice 21 (2023): 233–264.

A peer-reviewed legal analysis arguing that the permanent separation of Ukrainian children from their families and national identity forms part of a broader, prosecutable plan to destroy the Ukrainian nation as such, applying the Genocide Convention's specific-intent standard cumulatively across deportation, identity erasure, and cultural destruction rather than treating child transfer as an isolated act.

14. Babel (with comment from Petro Andriushchenko, Advisor to the Mayor of Mariupol). 'The Occupiers Take Ukrainian Children to Russia, Destroy Their Ukrainian Identity, Grant Them Citizenship and Encourage Russians to Adopt Them. An explainer article working through the legal definitions of deportation and genocide as applied to Russia's child removal programme, citing Ukrainian Presidential Commissioner for Children's Rights Daria Herasymchuk's distinction between categories of deported children (those taken with a parent or guardian; orphaned or parentally-unsupported children; and those separated from parents during the invasion itself). Traces the origin of the practice to the 2014 'Train of Hope' adoption project in occupied Crimea, situating the 2022-era mass deportation as an intensification of an eight-year-old policy rather than a novel wartime phenomenon.

An extensive BBC investigation drawing on multiple first-hand accounts of civilians processed through the Donetsk Oblast filtration system, including detailed testimony of beatings, electric-shock torture, and interrogation premised on accusations of 'Nazi' sympathies for using pro-Ukrainian language online. Cited extensively in the Yale HRL filtration mapping report as corroborating evidence for the Bezimenne, Nikolske, and Starobesheve filtration sites.

A peer-reviewed ethnopolitics article analysing the specific institutional design of patriotic education programmes in the DPR and LPR between 2015 and the full-scale invasion, examining how Russia promoted and resourced these programmes as a deliberate instrument of citizen-soldier formation rather than incidental ideological drift — a direct scholarly complement to the 'Forging Frontline Russians' and Jamestown literature on Donbas youth militarisation elsewhere in this section, with a sharper focus on the 2014–2022 institutional mechanics specifically.

17. Beard, Nadia. 'Ukrainians Who Fled to Georgia Reveal Details of Russia's 'Filtration Camps.'' The Guardian, June 12, 2022.

Documents the extensive biometric data collection occurring at filtration sites — palm prints, facial photographs, fingerprints, phone IMEI numbers, and downloaded contact lists — building a surveillance dataset on civilians passing through occupied Donetsk Oblast, including families with children traversing the same checkpoints and registration points used to process unaccompanied minors.

18. Bring Kids Back UA. White Paper on Reintegration of Children Who Survived Deportation and Forced Displacement. 2024.

Hosts individual named testimonies of returned children including Oleksandr (16, tortured with electrocution), Mark (13, mother imprisoned), and Kira (11, nearly adopted in Russia).

20. CCE Almenda. 'Russian Propaganda Camps': Where and Why Russia Takes Children from Temporarily Occupied Territories. Kyiv: CCE 'Almenda', 2024.

Geographically comprehensive mapping of camps across all federal districts of Russia to which children from occupied Ukrainian territories are transported. Argues the displacement of children to camps deep within Russian territory meets the legal threshold for deportation under international law. The definitive source on the geographic scope of Russia's child deportation network. Subsequent academic citation of this report (e.g., McGlynn and Romaniuk, elsewhere in this section) draws on its facility-level detail, including documentation of AK-74 rifle-assembly training and hand-to-hand combat drills for children at a facility in Astrakhan region.

The Ukrainian NGO, operating in cooperation with the Office of the Prosecutor General of Ukraine and Polish partners, whose database of 178 legally-admissible witness testimonies forms the primary empirical dataset underlying McGlynn and Romaniuk's peer-reviewed 'Capturing the Minds' analysis elsewhere in this section — included here as the documentary infrastructure source for that article's findings.

Notable as an official Ukrainian government assessment produced jointly with the President's Office initiative responsible for child-return diplomacy, rather than an NGO or academic source.

Documents the case of the 16-year-old son of the head of the Zaporizhzhia District Administration, abducted from a vehicle at the Vasylivka highway filtration checkpoint and held for three months, during which he was forced to clean up blood spilled during the torture of other detainees — direct evidence that minors were not merely processed through but actively detained and abused within the adult filtration and detention system, with no distinct legal protections applied.

24. Denisova, Lyudmila. 'About 'Filtration' Camps.' The Odessa Journal, May 6, 2022.

An account by Ukraine's then-Ombudswoman for Human Rights of the Donetsk Oblast filtration system, including her claim that approximately 2,161 orphans, children lacking legal status, or children without parental care had been deported by early May 2022, and her description of the practice as constituting genocide given Russia's parallel facilitation of their adoption into Russian families.

25. Dudko, Oksana. 'A Conceptual Limbo of Genocide: Russian Rhetoric, Mass Atrocities in Ukraine, and the Current Definition's Limits.' Canadian Slavonic Papers 64, no. 2–3 (2022): 133–145.

A peer-reviewed article, published the same day mass graves were discovered in liberated Izium, examining the semantic and legal instability of 'genocide' as applied to Russia's war on Ukraine, including the forced transfer of children as one site where multiple, often conflicting usages of the term collide. Widely cited across the subsequent legal and political-science literature on Russia's actions in Ukraine (including several gender-studies and security-studies articles cited elsewhere in this section), it provides the critical-conceptual counterpoint to the more straightforwardly affirmative genocide-classification literature (Ioffe, Khen, Muelrath, Mohammadi and Laridashti) represented throughout this bibliography.

26. Dutko, Alyona. 'Protection of Children's Rights in the Conditions of Russia's Armed Aggression against Ukraine.' Teka Komisji Prawniczej PAN Oddzial w Lublinie 16, no. 2 (2023): 47–58.

A peer-reviewed Polish Academy of Sciences law-journal article analysing the domestic and international legal frameworks available for protecting Ukrainian children's rights during Russia's full-scale invasion, providing a Polish legal-academic perspective complementing the Ukrainian, US, and Western European scholarship that dominates this section's legal literature.

27. East Human Rights Group (VPG / Vostochnaya Pravozashchitnaya Gruppa). Na TOT Ukrainy Dity ta Litni Liudy Zaluc͏eni do Zabezpechennia Viiny [In the Temporarily Occupied Territories of Ukraine, Children and the Elderly Are Being Drawn into War Provisioning]. 1 May 2026.

A short-form monitoring bulletin documenting that schoolchildren in occupied Kherson Oblast are being made to weave camouflage netting and produce trench candles for Russian military units, while residents of geriatric care homes are similarly engaged in the same war-provisioning production, with organised groups of pensioners in Novotroitske manufacturing trench candles as a regular activity. Notable for documenting that the instrumentalisation of civilians — including children — for direct war-material production, mediated through schools and social-service institutions, extends to both ends of the age spectrum simultaneously, and that the report frames this as the institutionalisation of population participation in war and a mechanism for building a long-term mobilisation-ready society.

28. East SOS (Skhid SOS). Lutsenko, N.V. Porushennia Mizhnarodnoho Humanitarnoho Prava u Sferi Osvity v Ukraini (Liutyi 2022 – Traven 2025) [Violations of International Humanitarian Law in the Field of Education in Ukraine (February 2022 – May 2025)]. Project team includes Vladyslav Havrylov. Kyiv: Skhid SOS, 2025. ISBN 978-617-7027-09-5.

A 43-page analytical report based on 162 in-depth interviews with teachers, psychologists, parents, children, and other civilians, documenting systematic Russian violations of IHL in the education sector in temporarily occupied territory: deliberate attacks on schools, the use of educational buildings as military command posts and unlawful civilian detention sites, the persecution and filtration of Ukrainian teachers, the covert deportation of children under the guise of evacuation (including named cases of children placed in Russian orphanages after the deaths of parents), and the systematic imposition of Russian curricula, the 'Conversations About the Important' propaganda lessons, and Yunarmiya-style militarised youth organisations. The report's named witness testimony — including a teacher's account of a father learning of his children's deaths, and survivor accounts of forced curriculum Russification dating to 2014 — provides primary-source documentation supporting the legal classification of these acts as breaches of the Fourth Geneva Convention (Articles 27, 32, 49, 50, 147) and the Rome Statute.

29. Eastern-Ukrainian Centre for Civic Initiatives (EUCCI), for the Coalition 'Justice for Peace in Donbas'. Involvement of Children in Armed Formations during the Military Conflict in the East of Ukraine. Kyiv: EUCCI, 2016.

The first systematic civil-society monitoring report on child recruitment into illegal armed formations in non-government-controlled Donetsk and Luhansk, documenting 37 verified cases of children participating in groups including the 'Berkut' special unit, the international brigade 'Pyatnashka', the 'Vostok' battalion, and the Union of Donbas Volunteers, against only 4 verified cases on Ukrainian-government-controlled territory. Documents both direct participation (children serving armed at checkpoints) and indirect roles (espionage, message-carrying, supply work), and explicitly distinguishes nominally 'voluntary' participation from cases where the appearance of choice was produced by adult coercion — a methodological and substantive precedent for all post-2022 reporting on child militarisation in this section.

30. Educational Human Rights House — Chernihiv (EHRH). Destroyed Schools in Sumy Region: The Story of 16 Educational Institutions. Chernihiv: EHRH, 14 January 2024.

A monitoring-mission report under the 'Education Under Fire' project (Kherson and Sumy regions) documenting the destruction, looting, and military occupation of sixteen schools, kindergartens, and vocational colleges in Sumy Oblast, including the deaths of school staff and the conversion of educational premises into Russian military positions. While focused on infrastructure rather than direct child militarisation, it documents the systematic disruption of Ukrainian-language education in border areas that forms the domestic counterpart to the deportation and re-education network catalogued elsewhere in this section.

31. Embassy of Ukraine to Ireland (Ministry of Foreign Affairs of Ukraine). On Illegal Adoption of the Orphaned Children of Ukrainian Nationality in Crimea. Dublin: Embassy of Ukraine to Ireland, 17 October 2014.

A contemporaneous 2014 Ukrainian Foreign Ministry diplomatic communication — issued within months of the Crimean annexation — formally protesting the 'Train of Hope — Crimea' adoption programme as a violation of Article 21 of the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child and Article 283 of Ukraine's Family Code, which require state authorisation for the adoption of Ukrainian children by foreign nationals. The earliest dated primary-source document in this section, establishing that Ukraine's government identified and formally objected to Russia's child-adoption practices in occupied territory within the first year of occupation, eight years before the issue reached the International Criminal Court.

A Ukrainian-language opinion column published on International Children's Day, surveying the scale of child deportation (19,546 identified names as of the column's writing), the mechanics of the 'recreation'-camp pretext used to transport children to Belarus and the Russian Far East, and the psychological harm of prolonged uncertainty experienced by children held in temporary accommodation centres far from home. 33. Euronews (with Reuters). 'Stolen Children 'Marketplace': Russia Creates 'Catalogue' of Ukrainian Kids for Adoption.' Euronews. August 8, 2025.

Reports on a Russian-run online database of 294 Ukrainian children available for adoption, searchable and filterable by age, sex, eye and hair colour, and behavioural descriptors such as 'obedient' or 'disciplined,' most of them born in Luhansk before the region's occupation began in 2014. Cites the Institute for the Study of War's discovery of Kremlin planning documents dated 18 February 2022 — six days before the full-scale invasion — detailing plans to remove children from occupied Luhansk and Donetsk orphanages under the guise of 'humanitarian evacuation,' demonstrating that the 2022 programme was pre-planned rather than an improvised wartime response.

34. Farrenkopf, Paige, Caitlin N. Howarth, and Nathaniel A. Raymond et al. Ukraine's Stolen Children: Inside Russia's Network of Re-Education and Militarization. New Haven: Yale School of Public Health, September 16, 2025.

Documents that Ukrainian children have been transferred to at least 210 facilities inside Russia. Key finding: documented military training and paramilitary induction at multiple facilities, including the All-Russian Children's Center 'Change' photographed in formation April 2025 and a Pskov Oblast military base. Concludes Russia is operating 'a potentially unprecedented system' of large-scale re-education and military training facilities capable of holding tens of thousands of children.

35. Fedosiuk, Tetiana. The Stolen Children: How Russia Attempts to Kidnap Ukraine's Future. Tallinn: ICDS, February 2023. ISSN 2228-2076.

An analytical policy paper examining Russia's systematic program of abducting, re-educating, and assimilating Ukrainian children as a deliberate instrument of demographic warfare. Analyses the legal dimensions of the deportation including its potential qualification as genocide, and situates it within the broader historical and ideological framework of Russian imperialism.

36. Filipishyna, Anastasiia, and Nataliia Mezentsova. Return of Deported Ukrainian Children by Their Parents: Are There Any Risks of Being Accused of Wartime Collaboration? Kyiv: Ukrainian Helsinki Human Rights Union, 2023.

A legal analysis addressing an underexamined secondary harm of the deportation system: Ukrainian parents who travel into occupied territory or Russia itself to retrieve their deported children risk being investigated or prosecuted under Ukraine's wartime collaboration statutes for the act of entering occupied territory or engaging with occupation authorities to secure their child's release — a legal bind that the report argues compounds the original crime of deportation by penalising the very act of family reunification.

37. Frantsuz, Anatolii, Nataliia Stepanenko, and Dmytro Shevchenko. 'Abduction of Ukrainian Children during Full-Scale Invasion.' Journal of International Legal Communication 9, no. 2 (2023): 16–26.

A peer-reviewed legal article analysing the abduction of Ukrainian children against the framework of international children's rights standards, with the lead author building on his own 2017 pre-war article on observance of children's rights in conflict zones (co-authored with Stepanenko) — demonstrating direct scholarly continuity between pre-2022 Ukrainian legal academia's engagement with children-in-conflict issues and the post-2022 surge in literature on this topic represented throughout this section.

38. Freedom House, ZMINA, et al. Stolen Childhood: How the Belarusian Regime Is Erasing Ukrainian Children's Identity through Displacement, Re-education, and Militarization. Washington, DC and Kyiv: Freedom House and ZMINA, October 2024.

A joint Freedom House–ZMINA report dedicated specifically to Belarus's role in receiving and processing Ukrainian children, documenting at least 2,219 children transferred to Belarusian facilities for 'rehabilitation' by late 2023, with evidence that some children remained for extended periods and were enrolled in local Russian-language schools rather than returned on schedule — the most comprehensive single source on the Belarusian dimension of the deportation system, expanding the geographic and institutional scope beyond the Russia-only focus of most other sources in this section.

39. Fronek, Patricia, et al. 'The Taken Children of Ukraine.' Child and Family Social Work 28, no. 4 (2023): 1082–1092.

A peer-reviewed social work analysis situating Russia's coerced adoption of Ukrainian children within the history of forced adoption as a colonial and genocidal practice, with comparisons to Argentina's 'Stolen Babies' and Australian Aboriginal child removal. Provides the comparative and theoretical framework for understanding child deportation as a recognised form of cultural genocide with historical precedent in Soviet practice.

40. Getmanova, Daria, and Svitlana Matviyenko. 'Producing the Subject of Deportation: Filtration Processes during the Russia-Ukraine War.' Sociologica 16, no. 2 (2022): 239–252.

A peer-reviewed sociological essay, part of a special issue on 'Wartime Sociology,' theorising the filtration process as a subject-producing technology rather than merely a screening procedure — the most sociologically rigorous theoretical treatment of filtration itself in the literature, complementing the legal and human-rights documentary approach of the Yale HRL and Amnesty International filtration reports elsewhere in this section with a Foucauldian and postcolonial analytic register comparable to McGlynn and Romaniuk's later 'Capturing the Minds' framework.

41. Gittoes, Megan. Russia's Coercive Occupation of Ukraine. Policy Report. Washington, DC: New Lines Institute, May 2026.

A think-tank policy report tracing the individual life trajectories of named Ukrainian children recruited into Yunarmiya and other militarised youth movements through to combat death or service in pro-Russian paramilitary formations, including documented cases in occupied Donetsk and Crimea. Reviews over 200 Save Ukraine-documented cases of repatriated children, finding 43% had undergone militarisation, and reports Russian plans for militarised training camps specifically for girls. Cites Jamestown's Eurasia Daily Monitor extensively and situates child militarisation within a broader analysis of property seizure, conscription, and demographic engineering across occupied Ukraine.

Access source →

42. Global Rights Compliance (GRC). Rewriting Childhood: The Russification of Ukrainian Children. London: GRC, 19 September 2025.

A legal analysis arguing that the Russification system in temporarily occupied territories — indoctrination, militarisation, deportation, and adoption taken together — inflicts torture and inhuman treatment on Ukrainian children both as punishment for pro-Ukrainian views and as a form of psychological torture through forced exposure to propaganda. Proposes a roadmap for prosecuting the system holistically under existing international criminal law and human rights jurisprudence, including the crime of forced identity imposition by analogy to forced marriage as an 'other inhumane act.'

43. Greenhill, Kelly M. Weapons of Mass Migration: Forced Displacement, Coercion, and Foreign Policy. Ithaca: Cornell University Press, 2010, pp. 3–10.

The foundational political-science monograph establishing the concept of 'weaponised migration' — the strategic use of forced population displacement as a coercive foreign-policy instrument — cited by McGlynn and Romaniuk (elsewhere in this section) as a partial theoretical analogue for Russia's use of child deportation, while noting the Ukrainian case inverts the model's usual logic by focusing on forced prevention of return rather than displacement outward.

44. Grytsenko, Oksana, Stephen Kalin, and James Marson. 'Ukraine's Stolen Children Fight to Get Home: "This Is My Country."' The Wall Street Journal, 12 May 2024.

An international mainstream-media feature following individual deported children's own accounts of resisting Russification and asserting Ukrainian identity while in custody and after return, illustrating agency and resistance among deported children alongside the structural coercion documented in NGO and academic sources elsewhere in this section.

45. Gwara Media. 'Russia Creates 'Catalog' of Ukrainian Children from Occupied Territories for Adoption — NGO Head Calls It Child Trafficking of the XXI Century.' Gwara Media. Kharkiv: Gwara Media, August 8, 2025.

A Kharkiv-based independent newsroom's report on the same adoption-catalogue revelation, with additional direct quotation from Save Ukraine's Mykola Kuleba describing the practice as continuous with Russian child-removal policy dating to 2014 in Donetsk, Luhansk, and Crimea, rather than a new wartime phenomenon.

46. Havrylov, Vladyslav, et al. 'Russian War Criminals Involved in the Forced Deportation and Militarization of Ukrainian Children.' Where Are Our People (WAOP). November 17, 2025.

A detailed investigative report identifying specific Russian state officials responsible for child deportation and militarisation, including regional governors who personally received deported children and financed their transfer to camps. Documents the use of the Vladimir Putin University of Special Forces — evidence of deliberate planning for children's future as a mobilisation reserve.

47. Havrylov, Vladyslav. 'Russia's Mass Abduction of Ukrainian Children May Qualify as Genocide.' UkraineAlert, Atlantic Council, 27 July 2023.

An authored op-ed analysing the legal basis for classifying Russia's mass child abduction programme as genocide, surveying the April 2023 PACE resolution, July 2023 UK sanctions explicitly citing the goal of erasing Ukrainian cultural and national identity, and US Congressional testimony characterising the abductions as forcible Russification. Argues that the persistence of indoctrination programmes despite Russian claims of purely humanitarian intent undermines Moscow's official justification and points toward genocidal intent under the 1948 Genocide Convention's prohibition on forcibly transferring children of a protected group.
