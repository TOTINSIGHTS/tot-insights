Who Gets a Home? Queue Order and Selection in Mariupol's Housing Distributions, May to July 2026

TOT Insights and the Centre for the Study of Occupation. September 2026.
Key findings
  • The queue is not worked in order. Matching all 5,822 households in Mariupol's compensation queue against the May and July 2026 housing distributions shows only 19.8% of the first 500 queue numbers reached, against 95.6% of numbers 3,501 to 4,000. Not one of the first 386 applicants appears in either list.
  • Membership in a block, not position in the queue, decides who is housed. May closed out cases under one resolution and worked the front and middle of the registry; July switched to a different resolution, opened a third, and covered the second half of the queue in bulk. Within each district the lists are sorted almost perfectly by queue number, but sorting happened after selection, not before it.
  • The published figures cannot verify how many families have been housed. The two lists print 5,223 rows but cover 4,810 unique applicants; 406 IDs recur in both with no status field, seven July IDs are not in the queue at all, and neither list records the address of the housing actually given.

How absent owners' flats become an "ownerless" municipal fund, and where the compensation housing comes from. This briefing follows the queue that the fund is meant to serve.

In July 2026 TOT Insights showed how the occupation administration in Mariupol converts the homes of absent owners into a municipal fund and redistributes them: to residents who lost their housing, to imported Russian personnel, and to developers. At that point the compensation queue stood at 5,822 households and the first distribution had reached 1,890 apartments. On 21 July the administration published a second distribution of 3,334 records. This briefing takes the queue as published on 27 May and matches every one of its 5,822 numbers against both distributions, to answer a simple question the administration's own communications never do: who is being housed, in what order, and by what rule?

What the documents show, and what they leave out

Three occupation documents underlie the analysis. The queue, as published on 27 May 2026, lists each applicant's queue number, an identifier, the regulatory basis for the claim and, for some entries, the number of rooms sought. The May distribution lists 1,889 identifiers. The July distribution, dated 21 July, lists 3,334 identifiers with the address and district of the housing each applicant lost. Neither distribution contains the address of the housing actually provided, so none of what follows can say which buildings people are being moved into. The documents establish who was selected and in what sequence, not where they went.

Three regulatory bases appear in the queue. Resolution No. 175 covers 2,422 applicants, Resolution No. 61-1 covers 2,659 and Resolution No. 141 covers 741. The three occupy almost non-overlapping ranges of the queue: No. 175 runs from about number 388 to 2,637, No. 61-1 from 2,639 to 5,563 and No. 141 from 4,694 to 5,656. The queue is therefore already stratified by category before any distribution takes place.

The missing first 386

No applicant numbered 1 to 386 appears in either distribution. The first queue number to appear anywhere is 387, and the first in the July list is 388. The first hundred positions are the clearest case: all are absent, and they include claims under both Resolution No. 175 and No. 61-1 and applicants seeking two-, three- and four-room housing as well as some with no room count recorded. The gap therefore cannot be explained by a single resolution or by a shortage of one type of apartment. It is the longest unbroken run of unhoused applicants in the registry, followed by the 166 consecutive numbers at its end, 5,657 to 5,822. The distributions cover mainly the central part of the queue and leave both its beginning and its final block almost untouched.

What the gap does and does not prove The documents do not say why numbers 1 to 386 are absent. Innocent explanations exist and cannot be excluded from the published lists alone: the earliest applicants may have been dealt with under a separate procedure that was never published, may have accepted repair or cash compensation instead, or may have withdrawn or been struck off. What can be said is that the administration has published no status for them, that the same applicants sat at the front of a queue it describes as the basis for allocation, and that applicants numbered above 5,000 were housed while they were not. The burden of explanation sits with the administration.

May and July did different jobs

The July distribution did not pick up where May left off. May was concentrated between about number 387 and 3,570 and contains nothing beyond number 4,000. July is shifted to the second half of the registry: only 23 people from the first 500 numbers, but 467 from the range 3,501 to 4,000, 461 from 4,001 to 4,500, 455 from 4,501 to 5,000 and 429 from 5,001 to 5,500. May was the main wave for Resolution No. 175. July was the main wave for No. 61-1 and the first wave of any kind for No. 141, which had been ignored in May and was still only half covered afterwards. Room count does not explain the pattern: the field is recorded mainly for No. 175 cases and is missing for 3,400 records, and the largest block of unhoused applicants, 712 people, has no room count at all. In practice the administration drew simultaneously on the late part of No. 175, the body of No. 61-1 and roughly half of No. 141, so that an applicant above number 5,000 could be housed in July while an applicant in the first few hundred was not.

Sorted by queue number, but not selected by it

Within each district block of the July list, applicants are ordered by queue number almost perfectly: the rank correlation between an applicant's position in the block and their queue number is 0.996 to 1.000. That is what makes the list look procedurally correct at first sight. But sorting a list is not the same as selecting it. The ordering organises a group that had already been chosen, and each district shows sharp breaks in the sequence, such as a case with queue number 5,512 appearing before case 452 in the central district, that resemble records added by hand to an already-sorted array.

The July list is built from district and address packages. The central and left-bank districts together account for 2,690 recipients, 80.7% of the list, drawn from 581 buildings across 187 streets. The fifteen largest buildings alone account for 436 people, 13.1% of the July distribution, and none of them belongs to a single resolution: the administration combined residents of the same building from different regulatory categories. Three thoroughfares, Metalurhiv avenue, Shevchenka boulevard and Morskyi boulevard, supply 717 recipients, 21.5% of the list.

The largest single building on the July list, Shevchenka boulevard 74, shows how this works. Its 38 recipients are all in the May queue, but their positions run from number 1,310 to 5,652, a spread of more than 4,300 places, and span all three regulatory categories. The most plausible reading is that the administration processed the building's residents as one case package and then listed the approved applications in queue order. The queue number sorted the cases; it did not select them.

What the lists cannot tell us

Four features of the documents mean their row counts should not be read as the number of apartments handed over.

  • 406 identifiers appear in both distributions, 271 under Resolution No. 175 and 135 under No. 61-1, scattered across the range 401 to 3,534 rather than clustered. The documents carry no status field, so it cannot be established whether a repeat is a republished decision, an unfinished case, a substituted apartment, a second approval, a duplication or inclusion in a cumulative list.
  • Seven July identifiers are not in the 27 May queue, five in the central district and two on the left bank, all sitting outside the normal sequence of their district blocks, and one in a different format from the rest. They may have been added after 27 May, corrected, omitted from the public queue or inserted by hand.
  • Neither list records where anyone was housed. The address given is that of the housing lost, so the buildings into which people are being moved, and whether they are new-builds, repaired stock or flats seized from absent owners, cannot be determined from these sources.
  • The row count overstates the outcome. The two lists print 5,223 rows for 4,810 unique applicants, and no public status exists for the 1,012 who are in neither.

Why it matters

The compensation queue is the administration's public proof that Mariupol's rebuilding is for Mariupol's residents. The July 2026 briefing showed where the housing comes from: a fund fed in part by flats reclassified as ownerless when their owners are absent, and a share of it reserved for imported Russian personnel. This analysis shows how the other end of the pipeline works. The system operates on at least three levels, the general registry, a regulatory sub-queue by resolution, and a district, address and case-file selection within it, so that an applicant's number in the general queue does not tell them when, or whether, they will be housed. What determines inclusion in the next distribution is membership in a block that the administration has chosen to open.

That structure has consequences. It gives officials discretion over who is housed while presenting the outcome as the mechanical working of a queue. It leaves the earliest applicants, who by definition lost their homes first, without any published status. It makes the headline figures unverifiable, because rows, unique applicants and apartments are three different numbers and only the first is published. And it turns a place in the queue into something to be negotiated rather than awaited, which is the condition under which informal payment and political loyalty become the currency of access. None of that can be proven from the lists alone. What the lists prove is that the queue is not being closed from top to bottom, and that the administration has not explained why.

Update, August 2026: a place on the list is provisional

In August the compensation programme moved from allocating apartments to re-examining allocations already made, and the same filters that decide who keeps a home in Mariupol began to decide who keeps a compensation flat. On 3 August the administration opened remote applications through Gosuslugi, Russia's state services portal, tying access to the programme to Russian digital identification. On the same day, recipients whose registration of a compensation apartment had been refused by Rosreestr were told to reapply to the Department of Property and Land Relations with a passport and a full set of documents. Formally a technical step; in practice an apartment is not treated as obtained until the right has been entered in the Russian registry.

On 17 August the administration annulled 21 decisions granting compensation apartments. The stated grounds were the absence of Russian citizenship, problems registering title, the recipient not actually being present, the apartment being rented out, or third parties living in it. More than 100 further decisions were scheduled for review. On 20 August recipients were reminded that within six months they must terminate their ownership of the lost property and land plot in favour of the occupation jurisdiction. Receipt of a new apartment is thus being used to extinguish the previous property right.

The scale makes the filtering significant. The declared compensation stock is 64 buildings with 4,778 apartments, plus six buildings with about 800 apartments reserved as service housing. As of 31 July the administration claimed 949 apartments issued from the municipal stock, 44 of them under a separate programme for people who had refused other forms of support and had no other housing; the July distribution analysed above added more than 3,300 records. Having expanded allocation sharply in June and July, the administration is now passing the same stock back through checks on citizenship, registration, residence and use. The Centre for the Study of Occupation's assessment is that the compensation stock is not property finally transferred to recipients but a managed resource: a decision can be reviewed, and a vacated apartment passed to the next applicant.

For the analysis above this closes one gap and opens another. Appearing on the May or July list was never the end of the process, which is one more reason the published row counts cannot be read as families housed. And the criteria now applied at the far end of the pipeline, Russian citizenship, Russian registration, physical presence, are the same ones that determine whether an absent owner's flat becomes "ownerless" at the near end.

Sources and method

This briefing is based on two analytical notes by the Centre for the Study of Occupation: one on the housing distribution published by the occupation administration of Mariupol on 21 July 2026, and one matching every number in the compensation queue, as published on 27 May 2026, against the May and July distributions. All figures originate in those three occupation documents and are presented as the administration's own claims rather than as independently verified fact, consistent with the Centre's methodology. Applicant identifiers and individual addresses are not reproduced. Coverage percentages are the share of queue numbers in a given range that appear in at least one distribution. The underlying documents and matching tables are held in the hub's private repository under its closed-monitoring policy and are not published alongside this briefing.

The August 2026 update draws on the Centre's monitoring of Mariupol for August 2026; the annulment, review and stock figures are the occupation administration's own statements.
