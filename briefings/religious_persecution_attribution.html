Religious Persecution, Cultural Destruction and Ukrainian Identity in Wartime: An Institutional Attribution Briefing

Vladyslav Havrylov, TOT Insights. September 2026.
Key findings
  • Persecution in the occupied territories is executed by five distinct institutional layers, not a single "occupation regime". The FSB, through its Centre for Combating Extremism (Centre E), and Rosgvardia identify targets and enforce; occupation "ministries of justice" and prosecutors run the re-registration and extremism-designation machinery that gives persecution a legal veneer; courts convert that machinery into custodial sentences; and the Russian Orthodox Church is the sole institutional beneficiary of the property and congregations the system displaces.
  • The mechanism is differentiated by design, and the differentiation is the evidence of intent. Five confessions face five distinct legal instruments: eparchial annexation for the Orthodox Church of Ukraine, prohibition and asset transfer by decree for the Greek Catholics, "foreign agent" and espionage framing for Protestants, a retroactively applied extremism ban for Jehovah's Witnesses, and terrorism prosecution for Crimean Tatar Muslims. One outcome: no institutional structure survives in occupied territory except the Russian Orthodox Church's.
  • Physical destruction is not, on its own, a reliable attribution signal. Of the 742 religious buildings damaged or destroyed by February 2026, over half belonged to the Ukrainian Orthodox Church simply because it held the largest parish network along the front line. Targeting is visible instead in which communities are permitted to rebuild, re-register and continue to exist administratively. The same logic runs through secular cultural heritage and language policy, and the identity it targets has moved in the opposite direction: Ukrainian as native language rose from 57% in 2012 to 76% in 2022, and civic identification as citizens of Ukraine first reached 85% across all regions.

Scope and method

This briefing covers all six occupied territories, Crimea and Sevastopol since 2014 and the occupied parts of Donetsk, Luhansk, Zaporizhzhia and Kherson oblasts since 2022, and asks which institutions carry out religious persecution, through what mechanisms, and to what end. It maps institutional actors only. Named individuals encountered during research (occupation officials, sentenced clergy, security-service personnel) have been routed to a separate list for the Occupation Officials Database rather than worked into the text below.

  • 1. IdentifyFSB Centre for Combating Extremism (Centre E): reclassifies religious practice as an extremism or security matter.
  • 2. EnforceRosgvardia: raids, seizures of premises declared "ownerless", physical presence at re-registration inspections.
  • 3. PaperOccupation "ministries" of justice and religious affairs: re-registration, the four-confession list, extremism designations.
  • 4. SentenceOccupation prosecutors and courts: convert FSB casework into custodial outcomes.
  • 5. ReceiveRussian Orthodox Church: absorbs eparchies, buildings and congregations the system displaces.

1. The security and legal apparatus

Three federal Russian institutions supply the coercive core of religious persecution in the occupied territories, operating in the same sequence in each documented case: identification, prosecution, adjudication.

The FSB, and specifically its Centre for Combating Extremism (Centre E), is the primary identification and interrogation body. Centre E's mandate, combating "extremism" under Russian federal law, is the institutional vehicle through which peaceful religious practice by Jehovah's Witnesses, Crimean Tatar Muslims and, increasingly, Protestant clergy is reclassified as a security matter rather than a religious one. This reclassification is what allows the FSB to conduct searches, interrogations and detentions of clergy and congregants without engaging religious-freedom protections at all: the case is framed, from the outset, as counter-extremism.

Rosgvardia provides the physical enforcement layer: raids on houses of worship, seizure of premises declared "ownerless", and the physical presence at re-registration inspections that gives the administrative process its coercive weight. Rosgvardia does not adjudicate; it enforces what the regulatory and judicial layers have already decided.

Occupation prosecutors' offices and courts convert FSB casework into custodial outcomes. As of late 2025, the Crimean Human Rights Group recorded at least 340 Ukrainian citizens held in places of deprivation of liberty in Crimea on politically or religiously motivated charges; the Crimean Tatar Resource Center separately recorded 447 residents of the peninsula under political persecution, 258 of them Crimean Tatars. At least 33 Crimean Jehovah's Witnesses have faced criminal prosecution on religious grounds, four of them sentenced in 2025 alone. These are not isolated prosecutorial decisions. They are the output of a standing pipeline that runs from Centre E's initial designation through to a court sentence, with the occupation "ministries" handling religious affairs supplying the paperwork that makes each stage look procedurally regular.

Occupation "ministries" of religious affairs, administrative bodies of the self-proclaimed entities in Donetsk and Luhansk and equivalent departments within the Russian-installed regional administrations in Zaporizhzhia, Kherson, Crimea and Sevastopol, are the paper-generating layer. Since 2015 the self-proclaimed administrative entities have officially recognised only four "traditional" confessions, Moscow Patriarchate Orthodoxy, Catholicism, Islam and Judaism, with every other community defined by default as a "sect" subject to restriction. This four-confession list is the administrative fiction that makes selective persecution look like neutral licensing.

2. The regulatory mechanism

The choke point common to every confession is forced re-registration under Russian legislation, which in practice cannot be completed without the community's founders and legal representative acquiring Russian citizenship. Refusal to re-register is not treated as inaction; it is treated as grounds to declare the community's property "ownerless" and subject to confiscation by the occupation administration. This single mechanism does the work that would otherwise require an explicit ban. It does not prohibit a religion, it simply makes the community's continued legal existence conditional on an act, acquiring occupier citizenship, that many congregations and their leaders will not perform.

Layered on top of re-registration are two further instruments. Anti-extremism designation imports Russian federal court rulings wholesale into the occupied territories: the 2017 Russian Supreme Court ruling declaring Jehovah's Witnesses an extremist organisation was extended to Crimea without any Crimea-specific finding of fact, and Hizb ut-Tahrir, an organisation banned in Russia but not in Ukraine, supplies the charge sheet used against Crimean Tatar defendants, the majority of whom are prosecuted for association rather than any violent act. Missionary-activity restrictions, inherited from Russia's 2016 "Yarovaya law" framework, criminalise unlicensed preaching, evangelism and religious literature distribution, a mechanism aimed squarely at Protestant and evangelical communities, whose theology and practice depend on public proclamation in a way Orthodox liturgical practice does not.

The Ministry of Justice function, whether exercised by an actual "ministry" in the self-proclaimed entities or by the registration departments of the Russian-installed regional administrations, sits at the centre of this mechanism because re-registration approval or refusal is a Ministry of Justice-type decision in every case, regardless of which institution formally issues it.

3. The beneficiary institution

The Russian Orthodox Church is not a bystander to this system; it is its designated recipient. Three transfer routes move property, buildings and congregations to the Russian Orthodox Church.

Direct eparchial annexation. Despite the Ukrainian Orthodox Church's declaration of full independence from Moscow at its Council in Feofaniia on 27 May 2022, the Crimean eparchies (Simferopol, Dzhankoi and Feodosia) were placed under the Russian Orthodox Church's direct administration by 7 June 2022; the Rovenky eparchy (Luhansk region) followed on 13 October 2022, and the Berdiansk eparchy (Zaporizhzhia region) in May 2023, the latter despite its own bishop's initial opposition. By these transfers alone, the Russian Orthodox Church has absorbed at least 1,601 parishes and 23 monasteries since the start of the full-scale invasion.

Property reallocation following prohibition. Where a confession is banned outright rather than absorbed, the same occupation order that prohibits the confession typically directs its property to the occupation administration rather than to the Russian Orthodox Church directly. But the administration's subsequent allocation of church buildings for religious use has, in the documented Zaporizhzhia and Mariupol cases, gone disproportionately to Russian Orthodox and mosque facilities, not to the confession's own successors.

Selective reconstruction. In Mariupol, of 39 damaged houses of worship recorded by independent researchers, 21 had been repaired by October 2024. That reconstruction was concentrated on Russian Orthodox and mosque buildings, while communities whose houses of worship were destroyed or seized, including the city's only synagogue, had no functioning premises more than a year and a half after the siege ended. Reconstruction funding, in other words, functions as a second, quieter allocation mechanism alongside the legal one.

4. The targets, and what differentiates their treatment

The system does not apply one instrument to every non-Russian Orthodox confession. It applies a different instrument to each, calibrated to what that confession represents institutionally.

The Orthodox Church of Ukraine, the confession structurally closest to the Russian Orthodox Church in liturgy and organisation, and therefore the one whose elimination requires the most institutional labour, is targeted through eparchial annexation and canonical absorption, not outright banning. The last Orthodox Church of Ukraine church on the Crimean peninsula was destroyed in June 2024, after a decade of administrative attrition rather than a single prohibition order.

The Ukrainian Greek Catholic Church, canonically loyal to Rome and therefore incapable of absorption into the Russian Orthodox Church's structure, is targeted through outright prohibition and asset transfer by decree. The occupation administration in Russian-controlled Zaporizhzhia region banned Greek Catholic activity on 26 December 2022, ordering the transfer of the church's movable and immovable property and land to the administration's own disposal.

Protestant and evangelical communities, numerous, decentralised and dependent on public proclamation, are targeted through missionary-activity restrictions and "foreign agent" and espionage framing. More than 400 Baptist congregations alone ceased to exist in the first seven months of the full-scale invasion.

Jehovah's Witnesses, already banned nationwide in Russia since 2017, are targeted through direct extraterritorial application of that ban, requiring no Crimea-specific legal process at all.

Crimean Tatar Muslim communities, the confession most closely identified with a specific ethnic-national identity opposed to the occupation, are targeted through terrorism prosecution, the most severe instrument in the toolkit, predating the 2022 invasion by eight years and continuing to generate the largest number of religiously motivated custodial sentences of any confession covered here.

5. Why: the functional logic behind each institution

Pushing past "Russia is repressive" to a functional account, each institution in this system is doing a different job.

The FSB is eliminating an alternative loyalty structure. Its target selection tracks which confessions could plausibly organise sustained non-cooperation, Crimean Tatar Muslim networks and, increasingly, Protestant congregational structures, rather than which confessions are largest.

The Russian Orthodox Church is asserting jurisdiction. Eparchial annexation is a canonical-legal claim, not merely a property grab. It is the Church declaring that Ukrainian Orthodox territory reverts to Moscow's authority the moment Russian force controls it, a claim with no equivalent available to any other confession.

The occupation administrations are monetising and reallocating property. The "ownerless property" mechanism and selective reconstruction funding move fixed assets, buildings and land, to favoured institutions regardless of the confessional identity of the community that built them.

The re-registration and missionary-activity regime, taken together, are producing a compliant congregation. The requirement to acquire occupier citizenship to retain legal status is not designed primarily to close congregations down. A congregation that complies survives, but survives as a body whose legal existence is now conditional on the occupier's continued goodwill, which is the outcome the regime actually wants from the majority of communities that are too numerous or too embedded to eliminate outright.

Read together, these are not four unrelated abuses. They are one interlocking system in which a security service supplies designations, a legal-administrative layer converts designations into property and personnel decisions, courts supply the coercive backstop, and a single church absorbs what the system displaces.

6. Cultural destruction and national identity: the same logic, a wider target

The institutions and mechanisms mapped above are not confined to religious life. The same occupying state runs a parallel attribution problem over secular cultural heritage, language and national identity, and it is analytically the same system: an institution that destroys or seizes, an institution that supplies the doctrinal justification, and a demographic and administrative programme that installs a replacement in what is displaced.

Scale and independent corroboration. As of 10 June 2026, UNESCO had independently verified damage to 536 cultural sites across Ukraine: 154 religious sites, 280 historical or artistic buildings, 41 museums, 33 monuments, 22 libraries, 5 archaeological sites and 1 archive. Ukraine's Ministry of Culture and Strategic Communications applies a broader methodology and records 1,707 damaged cultural heritage sites and 2,503 damaged cultural infrastructure facilities in total, of which 513 have been completely destroyed. The two figures diverge because UNESCO's verification standard is stricter and excludes categories, cultural centres and art schools among them, that the Ukrainian government counts. The divergence is methodological, not contradictory, and the same pattern of corroboration through different standards appears in the religious-buildings data above. The single most symbolic case remains the bombing of the Mariupol Drama Theatre on 16 March 2022, which was sheltering an estimated 1,000 to 1,500 civilians and killed several hundred.

The demographic and administrative replacement programme. Erasure in the occupied territories is not confined to destroying what exists; it is paired with installing a replacement population and narrative. An estimated 5,000 to 10,000 residents of Buryatia, a Russian region where Buddhism is the majority faith, have fought in the war against Ukraine, and Buryatia ranks first among all Russian regions in war dead per capita, evidence that the demographic and manpower cost of the invasion is itself being displaced onto Russia's own ethnic and religious peripheries even as those same territories supply personnel who are then rotated through occupied Ukrainian territory. Curriculum replacement, the substitution of Ukrainian-language, Ukrainian-history schooling with a Russian federal curriculum and organisations such as the Russian Military-Historical Society's textbooks denying Ukrainian statehood, is documented in detail under the hub's Education and Militarisation theme and is not repeated here. The two themes describe the same institutional actor, the Russian state and its cultural and educational agencies, applied to two different age cohorts and target categories.

Resilience and reversal. Ukraine's cultural institutions have simultaneously functioned as sites of resilience rather than passive casualties: theatres, opera houses and choirs have sustained morale on the front line and documented Russian war crimes against children through commissioned works such as the Metropolitan Opera's Mother of Kherson. This resilience runs alongside a measurable shift in national identity that is the opposite of what an assimilation campaign is designed to produce. The share of Ukrainians who consider Ukrainian their native language rose from 57% in 2012 to 76% in March 2022; 73% of Ukrainian youth now use Ukrainian in daily life; the share of residents of southern and eastern Ukraine identifying solely with the Ukrainian nation rose from 60% in 2014 to 75% in 2024; and civic identification as "citizens of Ukraine first" reached 85% across all regions, including the South and East, by July 2022. Measured against its own stated objective, producing loyalty to a "Russian world" civilisational identity, the occupation's cultural and religious policy is failing by the state's own metrics, even as it succeeds at the narrower, achievable goal of destroying specific buildings, congregations and institutions it cannot convert.
