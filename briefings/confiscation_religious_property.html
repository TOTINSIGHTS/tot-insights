Confiscation of Religious and Private Property in the Occupied Territories of Ukraine: The Melitopol Case and the Broader Practice of the Russian Occupation Authorities

By Mykhailo Brytsyn, Religious Freedom Initiative, Mission Eurasia. Brief analytical report, 11 September 2026. Hosted on TOT Insights with permission.
Key finding, from the report
  • The seizure of church buildings in Melitopol is part of a broader system: repression of religious communities deemed disloyal is combined with the forced reassignment of churches, the seizure of private housing, and the redistribution of property and business assets. The "ownerless property" mechanism moves this practice from isolated abuses to a formalised occupation policy.

Partner publication This report was written by Mykhailo Brytsyn of the Religious Freedom Initiative at Mission Eurasia and is hosted on TOT Insights with the agreement of Mission Eurasia Ukraine. It is reproduced in full and in its original layout; the analysis and sourcing are the author's. Six pages. It documents the seizure of at least fifteen churches in Melitopol from 2022, the extension of the same practice to the private homes of pastors and clergy, the "ownerless property" procedures that OHCHR and BBC Verify have traced across more than 34,000 residential addresses, and the forced transfer of Ukrainian Orthodox parishes into the Russian Orthodox Church.

Cases covered: Melitopol (Grace Church, Word of Life Church, Melitopol Christian Church), Mariupol (Good Changes Church), Makiivka (New Life Church), Donetsk (Latter-day Saints meetinghouses), Yevpatoria (Exaltation of the Holy Cross Church of the OCU), Horlivka and Debaltseve (Kingdom Halls).

How this report relates to TOT Insights research

The report connects two strands of the hub's own work. Its account of church seizures and forced ecclesiastical reassignment sits alongside Controlling Faith by Force, the hub's briefing on the systematic suppression of religious communities across the occupied territories, and its data companion. Its treatment of "ownerless property" as the mechanism that turns expulsion into permanent dispossession, including the homes of clergy who have fled, is the same mechanism documented at city level in Taking Homes, Giving Homes and followed through the compensation queue in Who Gets a Home?. Read together, they show religious property, private housing and business assets being redistributed through one administrative process rather than three.

The report's quantitative indicators come from different bodies using different methods and periods (OHCHR, BBC Verify, the US State Department, Ukraine's Ministry of Foreign Affairs and Human Rights Without Frontiers). The author presents them separately and does not add them together, and readers citing the figures should do the same.
