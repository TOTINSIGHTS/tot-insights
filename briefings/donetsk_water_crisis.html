Once Every Three Days: The Water Crisis in Russian-Occupied Donetsk

TOT Insights Hub. August 2026. DOI: to be assigned on deposit.
Key findings
  • The scale is quantified and the worst in occupied Ukraine. Executive Summary

Since the summer of 2025, the Russian-occupied part of Donetsk oblast has lived through a water crisis that occupation officials themselves describe as unresolved and long-term. Across Donetsk city, Makiivka, Horlivka, Khartsyzk, Ilovaisk, Zuhres, and Yenakiieve, centralised supply has been rationed to a few hours a day or, more commonly, to one day in three, and in much of the private-housing sector it has stopped altogether. Water that does arrive is frequently rusty, brown, or unfit to use, and is billed at drinking-water tariffs.4

This briefing sets the occupation's account against residents' own words and against survey data, and reaches three conclusions. First, the crisis is structural: it stems from the loss of the region's main external water source, the collapse of local reservoirs, ageing infrastructure, and a replacement pipeline that became a vehicle for large-scale embezzlement rather than a solution. Second, it is being mismanaged: scarce water is visibly wasted on fountains and lawns, prices have been driven up by clumsy price caps, and the crisis is monetised through bottled-water sales and private delivery. Third, the occupation's dominant response is not repair but narrative control: blame is shifted onto Ukraine, complaints are dismissed or criminalised, and the crisis is written out of official human-rights reporting. The briefing draws on closed monitoring sources held in the TOT Insights closed repository: monthly monitoring of occupied Donetsk for June 2025 to January 2026, a four-oblast resident survey, and the occupation officials database, together with the DPR Ombudsman's own public reports.

I. A Crisis Three Years in the Making

The shortage is structural, not a run of isolated accidents. The Donetsk agglomeration historically depended on water piped from the Siverskyi Donets to Donbas canal, fed from territory now beyond occupation control. With that source disrupted, the system has fallen back on local reservoirs that are themselves failing: by November 2025 the Nyzhniokrynske reservoir, a principal source for Donetsk, Makiivka, and Khartsyzk, was reported to have dried to cracked mud and puddles, and the Zuhres and Olkhovske reservoirs had shallowed critically.7 Analysts and residents trace the acute shortage to three compounding factors: dried-up reservoirs, frequent breakdowns in ageing water infrastructure, and the uncontrolled discharge of mine water as the coalfield floods.10

The intended remedy became a symbol of the failure. The Don to Donbas pipeline, meant to carry water to Donetsk, was overseen by former Russian deputy defence minister Timur Ivanov, who in 2025 was sentenced to 13 years for embezzlement; investigators described kickbacks, fake reporting, and bribery. The first branch was built in haste with heavy budget funding, and did not deliver a stable supply.2 Two cautions are warranted here. Ivanov's conviction is a matter of public record, but the court case concerned defence-ministry embezzlement broadly rather than this pipeline specifically; the link between that corruption and the pipeline's failure was not mentioned by the occupation courts. What is documented is that the pipeline was built and did not resolve the shortage. The dysfunction cascades across the utility system: the Zuhres thermal power station, which supplies up to a third of the self-declared republic's energy, has been threatened with shutdown because the reservoirs it relies on for cooling have shallowed, and dry pipes stop sewage flushing, causing blockages and raw-waste flooding of basements.11

II. How Bad It Is

The measured picture

A resident survey of the four occupied oblasts places Donetsk in a category of its own. Only 8.7 percent of the Donetsk sub-sample report constant supply, against 32.7 percent in occupied Luhansk and 47.8 percent in both occupied Kherson and Zaporizhzhia. At the other end, 19.3 percent of Donetsk respondents report no water at all, roughly seven times the Luhansk figure and three to five times those for Kherson and Zaporizhzhia, with a further 22.2 percent without water most of the time and 40.9 percent on a fixed hourly schedule. Interruptions to electricity or water were named as a daily problem by 88.9 percent of Donetsk respondents, again the highest of the four.1

The lived picture

Those percentages describe a daily regime of scarcity. By July 2025 the standing schedule was water once every three days in Donetsk and Makiivka, and in June 2025 the head of the Horlivka administration announced that supply would be cut to one hour a day, warning that upper floors might receive nothing.5 Residents describe carrying water up stairs, collecting rainwater in pots and buckets, filtering it through surgical masks, capturing the drip from air-conditioning units, and running washing machines from basins fed by improvised pumps.12 In Makiivka people were filmed scooping water from a broken street pipe; in Donetsk, neighbours came to blows over accusations of hoarding or wasting water.6

Where water flows, its quality is often the next grievance. Residents report rusty, murky-brown, or foamy water that leaves a film on the skin and a layer of sediment in containers, supplied at unchanged drinking-water tariffs.13 The public-health consequences surfaced in September 2025, when local channels reported early cholera cases among children and raised fears of dysentery and hepatitis A amid unsanitary conditions and medicine shortages. These reports originate in occupation-territory social media and require independent corroboration, but they are consistent with the documented collapse of sanitation.14 Scarcity has also hardened into inequality: access now tracks the ability to pay, with households able to spend 50,000 to 70,000 rubles on tanks, pumps, or private wells securing relative comfort while others queue with jerry cans.15

The crisis is driving people out. Monitoring through late 2025 records water scarcity as a named driver of a fresh wave of departures, including a new category of seasonal "winter migrants" renting in Rostov and Taganrog when supply and heating fail together.16 In January 2026 the collapse became simultaneous: strikes on energy infrastructure cut power to about 1.2 million people, and residents faced "no water, no heating" in mid-winter while continuing to receive bills.8

III. Mismanagement, Corruption, and Waste

The occupation's handling of the crisis has compounded it. The clearest instance is the Ivanov corruption case, which turned the region's principal infrastructure fix into an extraction scheme (see Section I). Beyond it, the pattern is one of waste, monetisation, and misplaced blame.

Scarce water has been visibly squandered on display. Through the summer of 2025, fountains continued to run in Donetsk's Shcherbakov Park and lawns were watered while homes went dry, prompting a widely shared complaint: water for homes once every three days, but for fountains "as much as they want."17 Administrative measures have made access worse rather than better: after price caps were imposed, small vendors stopped selling water by the litre because they would sell at a loss, leaving only bottled water at up to four times the old price.6 Meanwhile the shortage is monetised, with water-delivery pitched openly as a fast-return business. The former DPR co-founder Andrey Purgin has gone further, accusing the head of the republic, Denis Pushilin, of controlling six bottled-water factories and profiting from the crisis. This is an unverified allegation from a partisan source, a founder sidelined from power in 2015, and no independent evidence for it has been established; it is cited here as an indication of elite in-fighting rather than as fact, though it is echoed by some pro-Russian commentators.18

Responsibility is routinely deflected onto residents. Occupation officials have blamed up to 90 percent of sewage blockages on residents dumping waste, mounted raids on households watering gardens, and framed a parallel fuel shortage as "panic buying."19 The "solutions" offered by officials underline the absence of a plan: an adviser to Pushilin suggested that taps on plastic water barrels would be thawed with gas burners in winter to stop them freezing, an admission, in effect, that centralised supply will not be restored.20

IV. The Official Narrative: Blame, Denial, and Silence

Occupation messaging treats the water crisis primarily as a reputational problem. The standing explanation is external: the shortage is attributed to a "water blockade by Ukrainian Nazis," a framing consistent with the wider pattern in Russian occupation media, in which utility failures across power, water, fuel, and heating are blamed on Ukrainian strikes and paired with rapid-repair claims rather than acknowledged as occupation failures.21 At a press conference, President Putin located the only "fundamental" solution in the future: restoring the Siverskyi Donets to Donbas canal, which he tied to the capture of Sloviansk, making a humanitarian outcome contingent on further conquest.22

Where blame does not suffice, the response turns to suppression. In September 2025 a DPR deputy, Valery Skorokhodov, dismissed residents' recorded appeals about the crisis as "complete lies" and demanded that the complainants themselves be investigated, and the administration was reported to be weighing criminal penalties, under "extremism" or "discrediting" charges, against media that reported on water disruptions.23 The most telling silence is bureaucratic. The DPR Ombudsman's weekly socio-humanitarian reports, read in full across five editions from December 2022 to March 2026, never break water out as a complaint category and never mention the crisis in their events summaries; even at the March 2026 peak, the nearest heading, "right to a favourable environment," recorded a single appeal. The occupation's own human-rights apparatus does not register the emergency at all.9

V. Prospects: No Fix Without Conquest

The evidence points to no near-term structural solution, on the occupation's own account. Pushilin has stated openly that the region will have to live with shortages for a long time, and that neither drilling for underground sources nor seawater desalination has worked.24 The construction minister, Vladimir Dubovka, has conceded that mine water and desalination are economically unfeasible, leaving only stopgaps: patching pipes, draining what remains in reservoirs, and trucking water in.25 The mitigation is real but marginal, a target of more than 130 tanker trucks, 43 water-distribution points at boiler houses, and short local pipelines and towers, all of which deepen dependence on federal money and Russian contractors without restoring supply.26 The only "fundamental" fixes floated are either military, the canal after Sloviansk, or fantastical, a proposal aired at the Russian Academy of Sciences to pipe water from the Pechora and Northern Dvina rivers in northern Russia.27 Local experts expected the winter of 2025 to 2026 to be the hardest since the full-scale invasion, because the reserve water sources used in earlier years are now exhausted.28

VI. Conclusions and Implications

The crisis is documented, dated, and quantified

Unlike many occupation-era harms, this one can be measured. Survey data, a month-by-month monitoring record, and officials' own admissions converge on the same picture: occupied Donetsk has the worst water access of any occupied oblast, with only a small minority on normal supply. The evidence base is strong enough to support advocacy and accountability work now.

Corruption is central, not incidental

The Don to Donbas pipeline was built without resolving the shortage, and its overseer was later imprisoned for large-scale embezzlement, so corruption sits squarely in the frame, even though the direct causal link between the two is the monitoring's inference rather than a court finding. The water system should be read alongside the Hub's other work on how occupation infrastructure spending is captured, rather than as a purely technical or war-caused failure.

Denial and repression are themselves evidence

Dismissing complaints as "lies," threatening reporters, and omitting water from human-rights reporting are discrete, attributable acts. The Ombudsman's non-recording of the crisis is a documented consequence of information control, and belongs in the evidentiary record on its own terms.

Water scarcity is an engine of displacement and appropriation

By pushing residents to leave for Russia while newcomers and those able to pay secure supply, the crisis feeds the same demographic reshaping and property appropriation documented elsewhere on the Hub. It should be tracked as part of that process, not in isolation.

Independent verification is the priority gap

The strongest claims here, on reservoir failure, water quality, and early disease cases, rest on occupation-territory sources and monitoring. Satellite analysis of reservoir levels, health-surveillance data, and confirmation of the survey's fieldwork dates would convert a well-evidenced account into an unimpeachable one.
